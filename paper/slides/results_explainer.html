Two Results on Longer Trains

The surrogate was trained only on trains of 10 to 80 cars, then tested on trains of 121 to 150 cars that it had never seen. This page explains what that test measured, what came out of it, and why the second result matters for the rest of the thesis.

Holds up Train speed stays accurate on the longer trains. Slide 8
Does not hold up How far each coupler is stretched drifts badly on the longer trains. Slide 9

What was tested

The surrogate predicts how a train moves over the next quarter of a second. To simulate a longer stretch of time, it is run again and again, each time starting from its own previous prediction. That repeated use is called a rollout. A rollout of 200 steps covers 50 seconds.

Small mistakes can pile up in a rollout, because every step starts from the last step's answer rather than from the truth. So each result is reported at two points:

One step
Start from the true state and predict a quarter second ahead. This measures raw accuracy.
200 steps
Start from the true state once, then let the model run on its own predictions for 50 seconds. This measures whether errors stay under control.
Baseline
A deliberately simple rival to beat. For one step it is "predict no change"; for 200 steps it is "hold the starting state and never update it". A model worth keeping must beat these by a wide margin.

Every number below is the worst vehicle (or coupler) in the train, taken as the typical value over many starting points. Worst-case is the right lens, because a safety limit cares about the one coupler that is most stressed, not the average.

Slide 8

Speed: accurate on trains up to 15 times longer

On the trains it was trained on, the model's speed error after 50 seconds is 0.066 m/s, which is 27 times better than the baseline. On the unseen long trains it is 0.118 m/s, still 11 times better than the baseline. The error roughly doubles on the longer trains, but it stays small and far below the baseline at every point in the rollout.

Why it works

The network uses one small set of weights for every car and every coupler, whatever the length of the train. A 150-car train does not need new weights; the same ones are simply applied more times. So nothing in the model is tied to the train lengths it happened to see in training.

Two training techniques were needed to keep 200-step rollouts stable: adding small random errors to the inputs during training, and training on the model's own earlier predictions. Either one alone was worse. Doubling the training time nearly halved the 200-step error, which means the model is not yet as good as it can get.

Slide 9

Coupler shape: fine on trained sizes, barely better than the baseline on long trains

Coupler shape means how far each coupler is stretched or compressed, measured in millimetres. It matters because coupler force comes directly from it: once a coupler has taken up its slack of about 20 mm, every extra millimetre of stretch adds force. The control chapter will keep coupler force under a safety limit, so it needs this number to be right.

So the model knows how fast a long train is going, but loses track of how its couplers are stretched. That is an uncomfortable combination for the control chapter, whose safety limit is on coupler force, on long trains, over long stretches of time.

Three explanations that were tested and ruled out

IdeaThe model can't "see" far enough along the train. Each step it passes information five cars in each direction. Maybe that is too short for a 150-car train.
TestIf so, errors should be worst far from where information enters. Measure the error along the train in four quarters.
ResultError is roughly even along the train (11.4, 8.8, 10.8 and 6.8 mm by quarter). Ruled out.
IdeaThe whole train slowly stretches. A small, steady bias could add up to a large error over 200 steps.
TestForce the model's corrections to have no overall stretch, and retrain.
ResultLong trains: no change (39.3 mm either way). Trained sizes: about 47% worse. Ruled out.
IdeaThe shape error comes from the speed error. Stretch builds up from the difference in speed between neighbouring cars, so a small speed error could feed it.
TestGive the model the perfectly correct speeds at every step and see how much shape error is left.
ResultOnly 9% of the shape error went away (44.2 to 40.1 mm). Ruled out.

What it actually is: errors that reinforce instead of cancel

Here is the useful clue. At a single step, the model is almost as accurate on long trains as on the trained sizes: its per-step error is only about 17% larger. The difference appears over many steps.

If each step's small error were independent of the others, like coin flips, they would partly cancel and the total would grow at a predictable, modest rate. Comparing each rollout against that expectation shows two opposite behaviours:

On trains of a familiar length, the model's mistakes correct each other: a coupler stretched a little too far in one step tends to be pulled back in the next. On the long trains, the mistakes push in the same direction and build on each other. In technical terms, the model's learned dynamics are stable inside the range of train lengths it was trained on and not outside it. This is a stability problem, not an accuracy problem, which is why fixes aimed at accuracy did not help.

What this means for the thesis

Speed
The claim that the model works on trains longer than any it was trained on holds for speed, and the thesis will state it for speed.
Coupler shape
The same claim does not hold for coupler shape over long rollouts. The thesis will state this plainly as a limitation rather than leave it out.
Control
The control demonstration does not need to extrapolate to unseen lengths. It can train on the full range of train lengths it will control, and hold out routes instead. That avoids the weakness shown here.

All numbers are from the current training dataset (data/v2). That dataset is being rebuilt on a corrected, larger set of routes, and these results will be re-run on it. The design conclusions are not expected to change; the exact numbers will.
